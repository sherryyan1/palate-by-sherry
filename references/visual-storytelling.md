# Visual storytelling

A useful graphic helps the audience see something they would otherwise have to assemble from sentences: the objects involved, what changes between conditions, how a system is organized or where a method takes its inputs. A row of labeled boxes can organize that explanation without making it visual. **palate** therefore judges a graphic by the understanding it contributes, rather than by the presence of shapes, arrows or icons.

Use this guide when creating slides or reviewing explanations dominated by words, including pages that already contain a diagram or chart. The [design system](design-system.md) supplies typography, spacing and alignment requirements; [scientific fidelity](scientific-fidelity.md) governs what the visual may claim.

## Review every slide as a complete composition

Every slide within the requested creation or refinement scope deserves a finished composition in which text and visuals work together. Review the opening, context, question, method, results, transitions, ending and any supplementary pages that belong to the task. A strong cover or a consistent template does not excuse underdeveloped pages elsewhere. This requirement concerns the quality of each explanation, not an identical layout or a fixed number of pictures.

Choose the visual treatment from the page's purpose: evidence with annotation, a substantive scientific schematic, an illustrated comparison, a well-designed table or another source-supported representation. Give the text enough hierarchy and space to explain that visual, retaining the method and qualifications the audience needs. A decorative icon attached to a heading or text cards alone do not complete an otherwise unresolved scientific explanation. Exact mathematics, protected quotations and reference material can justify a typography-led treatment when imagery would add no understanding; record that page-specific reason and still finish its hierarchy, alignment and spacing carefully. Never invent evidence or relationships to satisfy a visual quota.

Review the sequence as well as individual pages. Reuse the same visual vocabulary without making every page the same, and keep [retained text fixed](spacing-and-hierarchy.md#keep-retained-text-fixed-across-slide-changes) when it appears on consecutive slides. Introduce new material around those stable elements rather than moving an existing explanation to create room. This continuity check is separate from whether each page looks balanced in isolation.

## Outline, then storyboard, then build

For a new or substantially rebuilt research talk, develop the argument first, then design the visual explanation for each slide, and only then construct the native slides. A polished box layout chosen before the scientific explanation tends to reduce different ideas to the same diagram. The intermediate storyboard must specify a composition that can actually be built, rather than simply promise an infographic.

1. **Outline the argument.** Establish the question, the necessary concepts, the source-supported method and findings, and the qualified contribution. Preserve enough detail for the selected audience and duration. Choose slide boundaries around explanatory purposes, not a fixed number of bullets or panels.
2. **Design each page's explanation.** Sketch or specify its research objects, their relevant states or differences, the relationship to show, the evidence to retain, the supporting wording and their spatial arrangement. Identify what the audience learns by looking at the graphic. Choose the [illustration language](#choose-the-illustration-language) from that purpose and specify its silhouette, palette, viewpoint and level of detail. Select or construct assets at this stage; an icon filename is not a diagram specification. Plan repeated objects and text positions across adjacent slides.
3. **Construct and compare.** Reproduce that storyboard as actual slides with native labels, framing and connections wherever feasible. Review the finished page against both its source and storyboard. Repair missing visual detail, lost scientific substance and inaccurate connections before proceeding to final delivery.

For substantive introduction pages, use richly illustrated explanations throughout: recognizable research objects combined with meaningful relationships, comparisons, evidence and enough nearby text to follow the reasoning. The introduction should let the audience see the phenomenon, gap, question and significance. Plain text containers connected by arrows, even with a small topic icon in each, are not an adequate default. Covers, contents and section dividers have different jobs; exact quotations or mathematics may retain a justified typography-led treatment.

The following compositions describe explanatory roles rather than fixed templates. Change their orientation, counts, proportions and assets to fit the study. Several can be combined on one page when the reading order remains clear, or developed across a sequence when the detail needs space.

| Explanatory purpose | Composition and useful detail |
| :--- | :--- |
| Introduce a concept | Pair an illustrated phenomenon or mechanism with a precise definition and source-backed scale or example. Show what changes in the research object, rather than only naming successive topics. |
| Frame the research question | Connect the illustrated motivation to a dominant question and meaningful analytical dimensions. Keep the motivation visible while the question becomes more specific. |
| Explain why an assumption matters | Align the conventional account and the added mechanism, keeping their shared starting point recognizable. Pair the contrast with relevant magnitude, operating conditions or consequences supported by the source. |
| Develop the wider significance | Use an illustrated system relationship above coordinated implications, each with its own explanatory detail; an optional synthesis band can connect those implications to the audience's research. |
| Explain the contribution | Compose distinct regions for what enters the method, what comparison changes, what is quantified and why the result matters. A region may combine a scene, a small evidence plot, short method statements and a qualified interpretation. Do not force equal content or four identical cards. |
| Connect background with evidence | Pair a mechanism or conceptual account with measured or projected evidence, distinguishing their status and keeping relevant scales and qualifications visible. |
| Explain interacting timescales or system layers | Align corresponding states across layers and mark the supported transfers, delays or constraints. Distinguish conceptual trajectories from data; never invent numerical profiles. |
| Explain an analytical procedure | Show the actual selection, matching, transformation or comparison with source-supported sample units, variables and intermediate states. Keep the operation visible rather than reducing it to an input-model-output chain. |

Richness comes from complementary explanatory work: a scene identifies the system, a comparison reveals a difference, an annotation explains the operation, and evidence establishes the result. It does not come from increasing icon count or repeating captions as pictures. Use composed scientific illustrations, retained source figures and native analytical graphics as needed; the small bundled icon collection is a starting resource, not a limit on visual design. Preserve readability and scientific qualifications, and split coherent subtopics when the composition needs more room. Retain a private record of each page's visual purpose and essential visible detail so a minimal implementation cannot silently replace the storyboard.

For a dense method diagram, reconstruct its explanatory hierarchy before styling its boxes. Separate inputs, transformations, interacting modules and outputs when these are real distinctions in the method. Group related modules, make the important feedback paths visible and give secondary links less emphasis without hiding their meaning. Merge redundant routes only when their sources, destinations and semantics remain explicit. Choose a layered, branching, cyclic or spatial arrangement from the actual system rather than forcing everything into a linear chain. Short labels and icons can simplify small components while precise operations, equations, constraints and supporting explanations remain available at the appropriate level.

A rich overview can be followed by focused detail pages. Keep the shared system, labels and meaningful positions stable while highlighting or developing the current part. Progressive revelation should help listeners understand the full explanation, not remove its substance or make unchanged text jump. When an illustration communicates a broad pathway, retain the technical mechanism, evidence and qualifications elsewhere in the visible sequence at the depth the brief requires.

## Choose the illustration language

Introduction and Methodology use two preferred illustration languages. Both use clean flat forms, restrained color and deliberate integration with the slide. Choose between them by the explanation the audience needs, with the section providing a starting preference rather than a fixed assignment. A polished surface must still carry the research-specific detail required by the storyboard.

| Language | Preferred role | Visual treatment | What the composition must do |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Freeform editorial illustration** | Introduction, topic framing, a high-level concept or a section-leading scene. | Flat vector-style artwork with rounded, refined forms, an organic outer silhouette and limited color. Compose it as a standalone cutout scene with open space around and within it. Avoid a rectangular image panel, rigid poster frame or arbitrary blob mask around boxed artwork. | Make the relevant object, setting or conceptual contrast recognizable and integrate it with the title and explanation. It should feel welcoming and substantial without becoming childish, generic corporate decoration or an unrelated scene. |
| **Clean infographic illustration** | Methodology, mechanisms, analytical operations and process explanation. | A modular system of explanatory mini-scenes, geometric components and supporting icons, with consistent line treatment. A slight isometric or semi-isometric viewpoint is optional when it clarifies an object; keep the rendering flat and lightweight. Blue-led color with yellow or other warm accents is suitable when it fits the deck and its scientific encodings. | Show what the source-supported components do: inputs, operations, intermediate states, constraints, comparisons and outputs as appropriate. Arrange them around the actual analytical logic rather than adding a decorative analyst, generic dashboard or input-model-output chain. |

Use freeform editorial illustration as the Introduction default, while retaining the phenomenon, gap, question, evidence and reasoning that the audience needs. An introductory slide that explains a mechanism can use the infographic language. Methodology normally uses the infographic language, but a brief scene that establishes the field setting may use the freeform treatment. On a mixed page, choose a dominant language and share its palette, line weight and object vocabulary across the components. Do not alternate styles mechanically by heading or combine incompatible drawing families merely because both are permitted.

Design the visible silhouette and placement before obtaining an asset. A transparent file can still look like a rectangular block if its objects fill a rigid perimeter. Keep cutout contours, internal openings and optical spacing intentional, and preserve proportions after placement. The preference against boxed illustration does not prohibit a necessary module boundary, a labeled comparison region or the axes of an evidence chart. Those structures must serve the explanation rather than frame every picture by default.

Describe asset requests through concrete properties: flat fills, restrained shading, consistent strokes, rounded or geometric forms, the intended viewpoint, the specific objects and the operation or distinction to show. Avoid glossy synthetic surfaces, heavy 3D rendering, fantasy-like detail, noisy textures and busy scenes. Decorative concept art and generic stock scenes fail when they could accompany any unrelated talk. Reuse recognizable objects across related slides and make their relevant change visible. Icons can identify components, but neither a row of icons nor a person beside a board explains a scientific method on its own.

Treat supplied style references as guidance for the drawing language, not as sources of research objects or data. Do not copy decorative bar heights, pie wedges, dashboard marks or implied relationships into a method diagram as findings. Use verified data for quantitative plots and native labels for the actual analysis. Keep equations, parameters, uncertainty and other interpretation-changing detail visible at the audience's required depth; simplify the drawing's surface rather than silently simplifying the science. Split an overfull explanation at coherent boundaries instead of shrinking it or removing essential operations.

Vector appearance and editable vector content are separate properties. Prefer suitable authorized vector artwork or native objects where the available workflow supports them, and keep scientific labels and meaningful connections native and editable. If an image-generation tool returns a PNG, it remains a raster illustration even when it resembles a vector drawing. Verify its resolution and edge quality at the placed size, preserve intended transparency and report image-interior editing limits. Do not flatten an entire methodological diagram into one bitmap simply to achieve a uniform style.

Record the selected language and its reason in the private storyboard. Review the actual slide for explanatory contribution, visual coherence, clean silhouettes, comfortable spacing and scientific completeness. A style label or a successful asset import does not establish those outcomes. The [cover guide](cover-art-direction.md) governs the separate title-cover composition.

## Choose what the visual needs to explain

Read the source before choosing an asset. Identify the research object, the distinction or action being explained, and the evidence supporting any relationship. Record that purpose briefly in the private slide specification alongside the source-to-output ledger. For example, a comparison of operating conditions may need the same physical system shown twice, with only the relevant condition changing. A generic symbol for the research field would contribute much less.

Keep three visual roles distinct:

| Role | Contribution | Scientific boundary |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Research evidence | Shows an observation, measurement, model output or source result. | Preserve data, scales, uncertainty, labels and attribution. A replacement requires verified evidence. |
| Explanatory schematic | Makes source-supported components, relationships or stages visible. | Check each connection and distinguish sequence, information flow and causality. Shape size and distance must not imply unverified quantities. |
| Illustrative depiction | Makes a source-described object, setting or intervention recognizable. | Identify a representative or conceptual depiction when it could be mistaken for an observation. It cannot establish a mechanism or result. |

An illustration may support a measured chart, but it does not replace that evidence. Likewise, adding a chart does not resolve a neighboring explanation that still asks listeners to decode a long verbal chain.

## Build around objects and relationships

Choose the composition before filling the page with prose. Use recognizable research objects where they clarify the subject, then arrange them to express the verified comparison, transformation or system structure. Keep concise labels and necessary qualifications nearby. Choose as many informative components as the explanation needs, organizing them into a clear hierarchy rather than attaching a symbol to every sentence.

| Explanatory task | Composition to consider | What makes it useful |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Explain an intervention | Show the relevant object, the intervention and its supported consequence. | The audience can identify what changes and what remains the same. Do not add an unverified causal link. |
| Compare conditions or mechanisms | Repeat a shared baseline with aligned views, changing only the relevant features, or use an appropriate [comparison composition](comparison-patterns.md). | The difference is visible without rereading two paragraphs; unequal baselines remain explicit. The available patterns do not constrain a new content-led design. |
| Explain time or operating context | Pair source-supported states with a timeline, clock or environmental cue. | The cue locates the explanation without introducing invented temporal profiles. |
| Introduce a complex method | Give an overview of inputs, modules and outputs, then develop the necessary detail on subsequent slides. | Objects and module identities remain recognizable as the explanation unfolds. |
| Motivate an analytical method | Show the available observations and the question or limitation the method addresses. | The method responds to the actual evidence gap, rather than an invented idealized dataset. |
| Summarize a research argument | Reuse the objects and relationships established earlier. | The audience can recall the reasoning through a stable visual vocabulary. |

Retain a flowchart, comparison table or equation when that is the clearest representation. Abstract methods do not always have useful physical counterparts, and exact wording can matter more than an illustration. Do not force a concrete metaphor onto a mathematical relationship or add icons merely to meet a quota.

Across related slides, reuse the same symbol for the same object. Keep viewpoint, level of detail, stroke weight and color roles coherent. Change a visual feature when its meaning changes, and use labels or other cues so color is not the only distinction. A microscope, network or field setting should serve the particular explanation; no topic symbol belongs on every slide in its field.

### Distinguish a symbol from a scientific schematic

An icon supplies a recognizable noun. A scientific schematic explains what the relevant objects do, how they interact, what changes between cases or how an analysis reaches its output. Compose those features at the resolution the source and audience need. A pictogram with an arrow to a conclusion is still underdeveloped when the missing operation or comparison is the point of the slide. Conversely, adding small parts that have no explanatory role makes a picture busier without making it more informative.

| Representation | Useful role | When it needs development |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| One instrument, organism or infrastructure symbol | Identifies the subject or locates a section. | A methods explanation needs the source-supported inputs, operations and outputs, rather than the instrument alone. |
| Repeated object symbols | Establishes shared identity across cases. | The audience needs to see which condition, component or response differs. Add that distinction; do not repeat identical symbols above different captions and call the comparison complete. |
| A composed scene or system | Shows source-described objects acting within a relevant setting. | Identify the interaction, transfer, intervention or constraint with precise labels. Do not leave the scientific work entirely to a paragraph below the picture. |
| An analytical diagram | Explains a model, experiment or comparison through its structure. | Make variables, processing steps, fixed versus varied factors or comparison pairings visible as appropriate. Generic boxes labeled input, model and output rarely provide sufficient methods detail. |

Icons can be components of a richer diagram, and a concise strip of small icons paired with labels can help orientation or recap. Keep those cues subordinate to the explanation; they do not substitute for its substance elsewhere in the sequence. Use object combinations, native relationships, annotated evidence and short explanations together when they help the audience follow the science. Do not require every slide to contain a physical scene or every panel to use the same arrangement.

When the source is text only, use its described system, experiment and analytical logic to construct a substantive schematic. Numerical data are necessary for reproducing quantitative results, but their absence does not prevent a well-supported explanation of a method or comparison. Preserve unknowns explicitly and leave unsupported result shapes, mechanisms and parameter values out. If the needed relationship itself is missing, identify that specific gap instead of replacing it with a plausible picture.

### Keep icons in a supporting role

Use icons mainly as compact recognition cues within a flowchart or immediately before a subheading or section label. Their job is to help listeners locate a component or recognize its role while the labels, operations and relationships carry the explanation. Do not enlarge a single icon to serve as the main illustration or fill unused space. When the page needs a substantial visual, develop a source-specific scene, schematic or evidence graphic with the detail its purpose requires. A composed illustration can occupy more space because it explains more; increasing an icon's size does not turn it into that illustration.

| Placement | Treatment |
| :--- | :--- |
| Inside a flowchart or method module | Place a small, relevant icon beside its input, operation or output label. Preserve room for the method detail and unambiguous connectors; the complete diagram remains the visual subject. |
| Before a subheading or section label | Align the icon optically with the associated text line, use a consistent gap and keep the wording dominant. Match the visible weight of equivalent icons rather than merely giving their files equal bounding boxes. |
| In a brief orientation or recap strip | Pair compact cues with labels and retain the substantive explanation or evidence they refer to. Do not turn the strip into a row of oversized topic symbols. |

Judge scale in the actual slide against the neighboring type, node and visual hierarchy rather than applying one fixed icon size to every canvas. Keep comparable roles consistent across slides and preserve legibility at presentation scale. Conventional scientific symbols and components of a detailed schematic retain the size needed to explain their relationships; this distinction does not justify presenting a lone generic topic icon as the main image. If an icon adds no useful identification, omit it. This guidance governs slide graphics, not the README heading icon system.

## Frame a research question

A Research Question page connects the phenomenon that motivates a study with the question the study investigates. Use this composition when introducing a study whose main question can be developed through related subquestions. Place it after the context needed to understand the problem and before the methods or findings that address it. An existing question page can fulfill that role; a tutorial, a narrowly scoped slide edit or a study with one indivisible question need not acquire an artificial set of subquestions.

The preferred starting arrangement has a narrow illustrated column at left and a wider question area at right. The left column explains how the problem arises. The right area gives the main question a dominant horizontal callout, then unfolds its analytical scope beneath it. Adapt this arrangement to the actual canvas and argument, preserving the reading order rather than the screenshot's exact coordinates.

| Region | Explanatory role | Adaptation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Left motivation strip | Show the relevant object, change, behavior or unresolved contrast that motivates the question. | Use composed depictions and short labels. Join stages only when the source supports their sequence or relationship; label a proposed pathway as proposed. A comparison or evidence-gap diagram can replace a linear chain. |
| Short framing line above the main question | Connect the observed problem with the study's analytical scope when that transition needs explanation. | Use specific wording from the research. A restrained accent line can establish hierarchy; omit the framing line when it merely repeats the question. |
| Main question callout | State one clear overarching research question, including the population, system, comparison or condition needed to understand it. | Use a wide, lightly filled rounded region with native editable text. A question-mark motif is optional. Keep the question visually dominant without turning an unresolved effect into an assumed result. |
| Subquestion regions below | Divide the main question into source-supported inquiries or analytical dimensions the study actually addresses. | Give each region a concise heading, a relevant visual cue and the substantive question. Align comparable regions and their text starts. Use as many regions as the argument needs, rather than forcing four equal cards. |

The left schematic should do explanatory work beyond identifying the topic. Its components and labels must reveal the source-supported motivation or contrast. The subquestion visuals can help identify inquiry domains; those cards establish scope and do not replace evidence, methods or result explanations later in the talk. Do not invent an outcome, direction of change, causal link or additional research objective to fill a visual slot. Preserve distinctions between the study's questions, its hypotheses, completed findings and future work.

Use the target deck's palette, illustration treatment and authorized branding. The reference's subject, geography, exact asset family, yellow accents, three stages and four cards are examples, not template requirements. Build callouts, cards, labels and connectors as native objects where feasible, keeping imported illustration interiors distinct from editable scientific content. Use readable text contrast rather than copying faint supporting text. Follow role-based anchoring: vertically center question callouts, filled heading bands and diagram-node text within their intended text regions; top-anchor unfilled explanations and free-standing labels. Match corresponding insets and preserve retained text positions across slide changes. Preserve title anchoring and the symmetric work area. If section navigation is useful, place it above the page title with centered labels at least 12 pt; the motivation strip itself is not section navigation. Use the target master or layout for recurring logos instead of copying a reference logo.

When a narrow canvas or substantial subquestions need more room, wrap the lower regions, move the motivation above the question or split the framing across a short sequence. Preserve the main-question-to-subquestion relationship and essential context without shrinking the text to keep a fixed grid. Review whether the audience can see why the question arises, identify the main question and distinguish what each subquestion investigates. Compare the actual wording and all visual relationships with the source before accepting the composition.

## Retain the substance of the explanation

Sherry's style uses visual structure to make substantial research content manageable. A slide can contain a composed illustration, several short method statements, a comparison and an interpretation when they serve one coherent question. The amount and kind of information in each region should follow its explanatory job. Equal panels do not require identical contents, and large empty areas are not a reason to remove information that makes a finding intelligible.

Before condensing a research explanation, identify what the audience needs to examine on the slide. Choose from the source material rather than completing a generic checklist with invented detail:

| Explanation | Content to keep visible when material to this talk |
| :--- | :--- |
| Method or model | Relevant inputs, key operation or relationship, outputs, study scope and the assumption needed to interpret that operation. |
| Experiment or comparison | What is held fixed, what varies, which cases are compared and how the comparison supports the stated inference. |
| Finding | Evidence, comparison baseline, direction and available magnitude, units, uncertainty and conditions that affect interpretation. A directional summary must not displace necessary source numbers or figures. |
| Contribution or implication | What was added or learned, the source-supported reason it matters and the scope or limitation of that implication. |

Keep these elements in a readable hierarchy: an entry statement, a useful diagram or evidence region, supporting explanation and a distinct interpretation where needed. Use aligned comparisons, nested groups, annotated diagrams or multiple coordinated panels before removing substance for a sparse layout. Preserve connected reasoning where short fragments would force the listener to reconstruct a missing step. A topic illustration and a broad conclusion alone do not establish sufficient detail.

For a short presentation, select a narrower coherent argument and explain its selected methods and findings adequately. Do not retain every section heading while reducing each section to a slogan. Track excluded branches and relocated material in the private ledger, and make a consequential scope change clear to the user. A supplied script may be retained in notes, but notes-only conservation does not prove that the visible explanation is complete. Move supporting derivations, secondary examples or the full original wording there only when the main narrative remains intelligible at the requested depth. Essential evidence and qualifications remain beside the claim.

## Select and construct assets deliberately

Native shapes, connectors, text and tables make the explanation easier to revise. Keep labels outside imported illustrations where feasible, and construct a diagram from separable components rather than embedding the whole explanation as an image. Preserve complex source figures when their reconstruction cannot be verified, reporting the limits on editing their interiors.

The optional [slide icon set](../assets/slide-icons/NOTICE.md) provides a small, coherent starting vocabulary. It is independent of the README icon system and does not prescribe a family for every presentation. Do not reduce an explanation to the symbols already available in that set. When they cannot convey the relevant features, construct a more specific native diagram, source a coherent licensed illustration or use a suitable illustration tool, according to the scientific purpose and available permissions. Keep its relationships and explanatory labels verifiable. Before importing an SVG into a presentation application, make a private use-copy with an explicit stroke color instead of relying on `currentColor`; verify the actual import and render. Keep the distributed originals and license intact.

A suitable illustration tool can supply a more specific depiction when simple symbols are insufficient. Inspect its geometry and scientific details, use adequate resolution at the final placed size, and avoid embedded generated lettering. Do not imitate low-resolution composites, mix unrelated icon styles or reproduce a reference's incidental colors merely because its explanatory composition is useful. Follow the user's authorized environment and [host workflow](host-workflows.md) before sending research to any additional service.

Use the [asset sourcing guide](asset-sourcing.md) for optional Flaticon search and licensed downloads, and for small labeled flags where they improve country comparisons. Use [figure panels](figure-panels.md) when a source composite needs to become separate evidence regions or a sequence of focused slides. Retain its scientific identities and shared interpretation context while making the surrounding composition editable.

Do not turn descriptive statistics into a plausible-looking density curve, draw a geographic distribution without geographic evidence, or invent trajectories to make a conceptual page look quantitative. If the source supplies only components and an unresolved relationship, show the components without a directional link and retain the uncertainty in words. Unsupported content is not repaired by labeling an otherwise misleading figure "conceptual."

## Give the explanation enough space

If useful objects, their relationships and readable text cannot share the work area comfortably, split at a conceptual boundary. An overview followed by a mechanism or module detail can preserve both orientation and depth. Give each resulting slide a clear explanatory purpose and map every claim, qualification, reference and note to its destination. Update navigation and cross-references after splitting.

Do not shrink below the 10.5 pt minimum, reduce section navigation labels below 12 pt, crowd a diagram to preserve an arbitrary slide count or split solely to make room for decoration. Keep unfilled body text top-anchored, flowchart nodes and filled text boxes vertically centered, retained text fixed and table alignment consistent with the [design system](design-system.md#text-boxes-and-tables).

## Close with visual key takeaways

Every complete presentation needs a final substantive summary that helps the audience recall what the research establishes and why it matters within its stated scope. Place a key takeaways slide or a short summary sequence at the end of the main narrative, before optional Q&A, contact details or supplementary material. A title can follow the presentation language and context; the visual synthesis is the requirement, not an English heading. Key takeaways need no dedicated navigation item. Use a truthful existing concluding group or omit the ribbon on the summary; keep any remaining ribbon within five items.

Build the summary from the messages already developed in the talk. Reuse recognizable research objects, a verified relationship or a concise piece of retained evidence, with short native explanations nearby. The visual continuity should help listeners recall the reasoning, while hierarchy and space make the closing message easy to grasp. Several lines of text, generic checkmarks or text cards with decorative icons are insufficient on their own.

| Closing task | Visual synthesis to consider |
| :--- | :--- |
| Recall distinct findings | Pair each supported message with the relevant object or evidence motif established earlier, giving the most important message a clear entry. |
| Explain the overall contribution | Reuse a verified mechanism or system overview, emphasizing what the presented evidence adds without turning presentation order into causality. |
| Compare outcomes | Retain a small, legible comparison or chart with its essential scales and qualifications, then state the supported distinction beside it. |
| Conclude a tutorial or proposed study | Summarize the concepts learned or the source-supported questions and next steps through an explanatory relationship or visual roadmap. Preserve proposed versus completed status. |

Choose the number of messages and the arrangement from the actual argument. Do not force three takeaways, equal cards or an icon for every point. Keep limitations and uncertainty that materially affect interpretation visible with the relevant message. Cite evidence as needed for a standalone closing page, and map any condensed statement to its earlier source and destination in the private ledger.

A closing summary must not introduce new results, a stronger causal claim, an unsupported recommendation or an invented future benefit. When a useful graphic cannot yet be authored or verified, keep that closing work pending rather than treating a text-only substitute as a completed visual summary. A request limited to individual slides does not require adding an unrelated ending, but a complete-deck task includes this review.

## Connect the intended objects

Every diagram arrow asserts a relationship. Record its source object, target object, direction and meaning before drawing it, distinguishing a causal pathway, sequence, transfer, association and proposed relationship. An attractive route does not justify an unsupported connection.

Build or reroute connectors after the nodes and labels have their final bounds. Prefer native object connections when available. Choose attachment sides and ports that match the reading direction, and place the arrow tip at the intended target's visible boundary with a small consistent clearance. Do not aim at a bounding-box center, stop in an unrelated gap, penetrate text or attach to transparent image padding. For irregular illustrations, use a deliberate visible anchor or explanatory callout. Account for group transforms, rotations, crops and scaling in the final slide coordinates.

| Check | Required outcome |
| :--- | :--- |
| Direction | The arrowhead is at the semantic destination. Verify the authoring API's endpoint convention; for Artifact Tool, `head` decorates `from` and `tail` decorates `to`, so a source-to-target arrow uses `tail`. |
| Attachment | Source and destination are unambiguous in the actual render. Inspect the visible object contour, not only the stored rectangle. |
| Routing | Lines avoid labels and unrelated nodes. Branches have deliberate junctions; crossings do not look like junctions. Feedback paths remain distinguishable from forward paths. |
| Filled nodes | Text is vertically centered within its intended region, with balanced clearance and readable wrapping. Horizontal alignment follows the text's role. |
| Final placement | Recheck every edge after moving, resizing, regrouping or splitting nodes and after native export. Recompute endpoints when the authoring tool does not maintain attachments. |

Inspect each diagram at full slide size and zoom into questionable endpoints in the delivery application's render. Fix wrong targets, reversed arrowheads, ambiguous crossings and gaps before acceptance. Generic overlap checks and successful file export do not certify connections. Record any unavailable native check as pending; the bounded preservation helpers do not perform automatic semantic routing or endpoint repair.

## Review the contribution, then the rendering

Review what the audience can now understand more readily. Can they recognize the relevant objects, compare the changed condition or follow a verified relationship? Momentarily looking past the words can expose a diagram that is still only a set of text containers. This is a diagnostic, not a requirement that a scientific figure explain itself without labels. A well-designed abstract flowchart may depend on precise terms; its arrangement must still make the structure easier to follow.

For a text-dominant explanation, accept the treatment only when it contributes the understanding required by that slide, or when a slide-specific reason justifies retaining prose. Recognizable objects alone can identify a topic without explaining a method, mechanism or result. Test whether the audience can locate the relevant operation, changed condition, comparison or inference in the actual composition, with its explanatory labels and supporting text. A generic topic icon, number badge, pale panel or arrow between unchanged paragraphs does not by itself meet that standard. Record the contribution or justified exception privately, alongside source support and any split destinations.

Review the visible content separately from the visual contribution. Compare the source with the main-slide sequence and explain any move to notes, appendix or exclusion from scope. Reject a polished treatment when the audience can read its conclusion but cannot find the source-supported method, evidence or reasoning needed to understand it. Also reject a detailed diagram whose added connections exceed the source. No icon count, word count, fixed panel count or automatic file check can replace these judgments.

Finally, inspect every resulting slide in the delivery application. Check scientific meaning, native editability and appearance separately, including connector endpoints, object identity, visible labels, image quality, contrast, spacing and work-area bounds. An automated asset or file check cannot establish that a graphic explains the research well, and unavailable native review remains pending.
