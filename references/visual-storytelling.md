# Visual storytelling

A useful graphic helps the audience see something they would otherwise have to assemble from sentences: the objects involved, what changes between conditions, how a system is organized or where a method takes its inputs. A row of labeled boxes can organize that explanation without making it visual. **palate** therefore judges a graphic by the understanding it contributes, rather than by the presence of shapes, arrows or icons.

Use this guide when creating slides or reviewing explanations dominated by words, including pages that already contain a diagram or chart. The [design system](design-system.md) supplies typography, spacing and alignment requirements; [scientific fidelity](scientific-fidelity.md) governs what the visual may claim.

## Choose what the visual needs to explain

Read the source before choosing an asset. Identify the research object, the distinction or action being explained, and the evidence supporting any relationship. Record that purpose briefly in the private slide specification alongside the source-to-output ledger. For example, a comparison of operating conditions may need the same physical system shown twice, with only the relevant condition changing. A generic symbol for the research field would contribute much less.

Keep three visual roles distinct:

| Role | Contribution | Scientific boundary |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Research evidence | Shows an observation, measurement, model output or source result. | Preserve data, scales, uncertainty, labels and attribution. A replacement requires verified evidence. |
| Explanatory schematic | Makes source-supported components, relationships or stages visible. | Check each connection and distinguish sequence, information flow and causality. Shape size and distance must not imply unverified quantities. |
| Illustrative depiction | Makes a source-described object, setting or intervention recognizable. | Identify a representative or conceptual depiction when it could be mistaken for an observation. It cannot establish a mechanism or result. |

An illustration may support a measured chart, but it does not replace that evidence. Likewise, adding a chart does not resolve a neighboring explanation that still asks listeners to decode a long verbal chain.

## Build around objects and relationships

Choose the composition before filling the page with prose. Use recognizable research objects where they clarify the subject, then arrange them to express the verified comparison, transformation or system structure. Keep concise labels and necessary qualifications nearby. A small number of informative objects is often more effective than a symbol attached to every sentence.

| Explanatory task | Composition to consider | What makes it useful |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Explain an intervention | Show the relevant object, the intervention and its supported consequence. | The audience can identify what changes and what remains the same. Do not add an unverified causal link. |
| Compare conditions or mechanisms | Repeat a shared baseline with aligned views, changing only the relevant features. | The difference is visible without rereading two paragraphs; unequal baselines remain explicit. |
| Explain time or operating context | Pair source-supported states with a timeline, clock or environmental cue. | The cue locates the explanation without introducing invented temporal profiles. |
| Introduce a complex method | Give an overview of inputs, modules and outputs, then develop the necessary detail on subsequent slides. | Objects and module identities remain recognizable as the explanation unfolds. |
| Motivate an analytical method | Show the available observations and the question or limitation the method addresses. | The method responds to the actual evidence gap, rather than an invented idealized dataset. |
| Summarize a research argument | Reuse the objects and relationships established earlier. | The audience can recall the reasoning through a stable visual vocabulary. |

Retain a flowchart, comparison table or equation when that is the clearest representation. Abstract methods do not always have useful physical counterparts, and exact wording can matter more than an illustration. Do not force a concrete metaphor onto a mathematical relationship or add icons merely to meet a quota.

Across related slides, reuse the same symbol for the same object. Keep viewpoint, level of detail, stroke weight and color roles coherent. Change a visual feature when its meaning changes, and use labels or other cues so color is not the only distinction. A microscope, network or field setting should serve the particular explanation; no topic symbol belongs on every slide in its field.

### Distinguish a symbol from a scientific schematic

An icon supplies a recognizable noun. A scientific schematic explains what the relevant objects do, how they interact, what changes between cases or how an analysis reaches its output. Compose those features at the resolution the source and audience need. A pictogram with an arrow to a conclusion is still underdeveloped when the missing operation or comparison is the point of the slide. Conversely, adding small parts that have no explanatory role makes a picture busier without making it more informative.

| Representation | Useful role | When it needs development |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| One instrument, organism or infrastructure symbol | Identifies the subject or locates a section. | A methods explanation needs the source-supported inputs, operations and outputs, rather than the instrument alone. |
| Repeated object symbols | Establishes shared identity across cases. | The audience needs to see which condition, component or response differs. Add that distinction; do not repeat identical symbols above different captions and call the comparison complete. |
| A composed scene or system | Shows source-described objects acting within a relevant setting. | Identify the interaction, transfer, intervention or constraint with precise labels. Do not leave the scientific work entirely to a paragraph below the picture. |
| An analytical diagram | Explains a model, experiment or comparison through its structure. | Make variables, processing steps, fixed versus varied factors or comparison pairings visible as appropriate. Generic boxes labeled input, model and output rarely provide sufficient methods detail. |

Icons can be components of a richer diagram, and a concise icon-led strip can help orientation or recap. Neither is a substitute for the substantive explanation elsewhere in the sequence. Use object combinations, native relationships, annotated evidence and short explanations together when they help the audience follow the science. Do not require every slide to contain a physical scene or every panel to use the same arrangement.

When the source is text only, use its described system, experiment and analytical logic to construct a substantive schematic. Numerical data are necessary for reproducing quantitative results, but their absence does not prevent a well-supported explanation of a method or comparison. Preserve unknowns explicitly and leave unsupported result shapes, mechanisms and parameter values out. If the needed relationship itself is missing, identify that specific gap instead of replacing it with a plausible picture.

## Retain the substance of the explanation

Sherry's style uses visual structure to make substantial research content manageable. A slide can contain a composed illustration, several short method statements, a comparison and an interpretation when they serve one coherent question. The amount and kind of information in each region should follow its explanatory job. Equal panels do not require identical contents, and large empty areas are not a reason to remove information that makes a finding intelligible.

Before condensing a research explanation, identify what the audience needs to examine on the slide. Choose from the source material rather than completing a generic checklist with invented detail:

| Explanation | Content to keep visible when material to this talk |
| :--- | :--- |
| Method or model | Relevant inputs, key operation or relationship, outputs, study scope and the assumption needed to interpret that operation. |
| Experiment or comparison | What is held fixed, what varies, which cases are compared and how the comparison supports the stated inference. |
| Finding | Evidence, comparison baseline, direction and available magnitude, units, uncertainty and conditions that affect interpretation. A directional summary must not displace necessary source numbers or figures. |
| Contribution or implication | What was added or learned, the source-supported reason it matters and the scope or limitation of that implication. |

Keep these elements in a readable hierarchy: an entry statement, a useful diagram or evidence region, supporting explanation and a distinct interpretation where needed. Use aligned comparisons, nested groups, annotated diagrams or multiple coordinated panels before removing substance for a sparse layout. Preserve connected reasoning where short fragments would force the listener to reconstruct a missing step. A topic illustration and a broad conclusion alone do not establish sufficient detail.

For a short presentation, select a narrower coherent argument and explain its selected methods and findings adequately. Do not retain every section heading while reducing each section to a slogan. Track excluded branches and relocated material in the private ledger, and make a consequential scope change clear to the user. A supplied script may be retained in notes, but notes-only conservation does not prove that the visible explanation is complete. Move supporting derivations, secondary examples or the full original wording there only when the main narrative remains intelligible at the requested depth. Essential evidence and qualifications remain beside the claim.

## Select and construct assets deliberately

Native shapes, connectors, text and tables make the explanation easier to revise. Keep labels outside imported illustrations where feasible, and construct a diagram from separable components rather than embedding the whole explanation as an image. Preserve complex source figures when their reconstruction cannot be verified, reporting the limits on editing their interiors.

The optional [slide icon set](../assets/slide-icons/NOTICE.md) provides a small, coherent starting vocabulary. It is independent of the README icon system and does not prescribe a family for every presentation. Do not reduce an explanation to the symbols already available in that set. When they cannot convey the relevant features, construct a more specific native diagram, source a coherent licensed illustration or use a suitable illustration tool, according to the scientific purpose and available permissions. Keep its relationships and explanatory labels verifiable. Before importing an SVG into a presentation application, make a private use-copy with an explicit stroke color instead of relying on `currentColor`; verify the actual import and render. Keep the distributed originals and license intact.

A suitable illustration tool can supply a more specific depiction when simple symbols are insufficient. Inspect its geometry and scientific details, use adequate resolution at the final placed size, and avoid embedded generated lettering. Do not imitate low-resolution composites, mix unrelated icon styles or reproduce a reference's incidental colors merely because its explanatory composition is useful. Follow the user's authorized environment and [host workflow](host-workflows.md) before sending research to any additional service.

Use the [asset sourcing guide](asset-sourcing.md) for optional Flaticon search and licensed downloads, and for small labeled flags where they improve country comparisons. Use [figure panels](figure-panels.md) when a source composite needs to become separate evidence regions or a sequence of focused slides. Retain its scientific identities and shared interpretation context while making the surrounding composition editable.

Do not turn descriptive statistics into a plausible-looking density curve, draw a geographic distribution without geographic evidence, or invent trajectories to make a conceptual page look quantitative. If the source supplies only components and an unresolved relationship, show the components without a directional link and retain the uncertainty in words. Unsupported content is not repaired by labeling an otherwise misleading figure "conceptual."

## Give the explanation enough space

If useful objects, their relationships and readable text cannot share the work area comfortably, split at a conceptual boundary. An overview followed by a mechanism or module detail can preserve both orientation and depth. Give each resulting slide a clear explanatory purpose and map every claim, qualification, reference and note to its destination. Update navigation and cross-references after splitting.

Do not shrink below the 10.5 pt minimum, reduce section navigation labels below 12 pt, crowd a diagram to preserve an arbitrary slide count or split solely to make room for decoration. Keep corresponding non-title text top-anchored, navigation labels vertically centered and table alignment consistent with the [design system](design-system.md#text-boxes-and-tables).

## Close with visual key takeaways

Every complete presentation needs a final substantive summary that helps the audience recall what the research establishes and why it matters within its stated scope. Place a key takeaways slide or a short summary sequence at the end of the main narrative, before optional Q&A, contact details or supplementary material. A title can follow the presentation language and context; the visual synthesis is the requirement, not an English heading.

Build the summary from the messages already developed in the talk. Reuse recognizable research objects, a verified relationship or a concise piece of retained evidence, with short native explanations nearby. The visual continuity should help listeners recall the reasoning, while hierarchy and space make the closing message easy to grasp. Several lines of text, generic checkmarks or text cards with decorative icons are insufficient on their own.

| Closing task | Visual synthesis to consider |
| :--- | :--- |
| Recall distinct findings | Pair each supported message with the relevant object or evidence motif established earlier, giving the most important message a clear entry. |
| Explain the overall contribution | Reuse a verified mechanism or system overview, emphasizing what the presented evidence adds without turning presentation order into causality. |
| Compare outcomes | Retain a small, legible comparison or chart with its essential scales and qualifications, then state the supported distinction beside it. |
| Conclude a tutorial or proposed study | Summarize the concepts learned or the source-supported questions and next steps through an explanatory relationship or visual roadmap. Preserve proposed versus completed status. |

Choose the number of messages and the arrangement from the actual argument. Do not force three takeaways, equal cards or an icon for every point. Keep limitations and uncertainty that materially affect interpretation visible with the relevant message. Cite evidence as needed for a standalone closing page, and map any condensed statement to its earlier source and destination in the private ledger.

A closing summary must not introduce new results, a stronger causal claim, an unsupported recommendation or an invented future benefit. When a useful graphic cannot yet be authored or verified, keep that closing work pending rather than treating a text-only substitute as a completed visual summary. A request limited to individual slides does not require adding an unrelated ending, but a complete-deck task includes this review.

## Review the contribution, then the rendering

Review what the audience can now understand more readily. Can they recognize the relevant objects, compare the changed condition or follow a verified relationship? Momentarily looking past the words can expose a diagram that is still only a set of text containers. This is a diagnostic, not a requirement that a scientific figure explain itself without labels. A well-designed abstract flowchart may depend on precise terms; its arrangement must still make the structure easier to follow.

For a text-dominant explanation, accept the treatment only when it contributes the understanding required by that slide, or when a slide-specific reason justifies retaining prose. Recognizable objects alone can identify a topic without explaining a method, mechanism or result. Test whether the audience can locate the relevant operation, changed condition, comparison or inference in the actual composition, with its explanatory labels and supporting text. A generic topic icon, number badge, pale panel or arrow between unchanged paragraphs does not by itself meet that standard. Record the contribution or justified exception privately, alongside source support and any split destinations.

Review the visible content separately from the visual contribution. Compare the source with the main-slide sequence and explain any move to notes, appendix or exclusion from scope. Reject a polished treatment when the audience can read its conclusion but cannot find the source-supported method, evidence or reasoning needed to understand it. Also reject a detailed diagram whose added connections exceed the source. No icon count, word count, fixed panel count or automatic file check can replace these judgments.

Finally, inspect every resulting slide in the delivery application. Check scientific meaning, native editability and appearance separately, including connector endpoints, object identity, visible labels, image quality, contrast, spacing and work-area bounds. An automated asset or file check cannot establish that a graphic explains the research well, and unavailable native review remains pending.
